OpenSAM Finds $4.3 Billion In Federal Contracts No One Told You About

A small open-source instrument, written in TypeScript and shipped under the MIT license, watches SAM.gov day and night — and quietly hands small businesses the contracts they were already eligible to win.

By A. Flores · OpenSAM Times Filed September 16, 2026

WASHINGTON, Sept. 16 — Every weekday morning before sunrise, the federal government's procurement portal, SAM.gov, drops thousands of new contracting opportunities into the public record. By the time the coffee is poured, the teams who knew where to look have already started work. OpenSAM, an open-source instrument released this year by AliceLabs LLC under the MIT license, was built for everyone else.[1]

It watches SAM.gov continuously, scores every opportunity against a small business's capabilities, and surfaces the contracts worth bidding on — usually in fewer than two hundred milliseconds. The scoring engine itself is one hundred and nineteen lines of TypeScript. It considers NAICS code match, set-aside alignment, capability keywords in the description, and deadline proximity. The result is a deterministic 0–100 score, with no machine learning, no probabilistic guessing, and no telemetry.

It is deliberately boring software. There is no analytics. There is no signup. There is a score and a list.

“We tracked more than four point three billion dollars in opportunities last quarter alone,” a person familiar with the matter said. “Most of them will never be seen by the small businesses they were set aside for.” The tool, which carries zero runtime dependencies, has been described by its maintainers as the kind of thing the federal market should have had for free a decade ago.[2]

Three packages ship today: @opensam/sdk, the application interface; @opensam/scoring, the engine; and @opensam/sam-gov-types, the type definitions for the SAM.gov API. All three are available on npm at no cost. The web app at opensam.us requires no signup, no email, and no payment method.

The project, the editors note, was not built to disrupt anything. It was built so that a one-person shop in Silver Spring, an eight(a) certified firm in El Paso, and a capture manager reading this on a phone in the Dulles parking lot can all see the same opportunities, at the same time, as the prime contractors who already pay six figures a year for the privilege.

Editorial · C2

Why We Built This

An editorial, in five numbered sections.

By The Editors September 16, 2026

§1. We built OpenSAM because the federal contracting market is, by design, opaque. The opportunities exist. The small-business set-asides exist. The dollars are appropriated. The barrier between a small business and its first contract is not capital. It is information.

§2. We do not believe a tool that finds federal contracts should cost six figures a year. We do not believe it should require a sales call, a demo, an enterprise agreement, or a quarterly review. The data is public. The tool should be public.[3]

§3. We do not believe a scoring engine should be a black box. Ours is one hundred and nineteen lines of TypeScript. You can read it in less time than it takes to read this editorial. We invite you to.[1]

§4. We do not collect telemetry. We do not know who uses OpenSAM. We do not want to know. The software runs on your machine. The opportunity is yours. The decision is yours.

§5. The MIT license is not a marketing strategy. It is a legal commitment to let you do whatever you want with this code, forever, including things we have not thought of. Especially those.

[1] The 119-line scoring engine is available at github.com/eddyflores100-lang/OpenSAM. Read it. We dare you. [2] “Zero dependencies” means zero packages in your node_modules that you did not put there yourself. Twenty-nine tests pass on every commit. [3] Built by AliceLabs LLC, an independent company. Letters to the Editor

Six letters, in the order they arrived. Lightly edited for length. Not for opinion.

From A reader in Silver Spring.

What is OpenSAM, in one sentence?

An open-source tool that finds, scores, and monitors federal contracting opportunities published on SAM.gov. That is the sentence.

From A capture manager in Arlington.

Is it really free?

Yes. MIT-licensed, zero cost. You will need a free api.data.gov key to read SAM.gov data, which is the government’s, not ours.

From A small-business owner, 8(a) certified.

Who is OpenSAM for?

Small businesses (8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, SDVOSB), capture and BD teams, AI agent builders, and researchers covering the federal procurement market.

From A non-developer in Boulder.

Do I have to write code?

No. The web app at opensam.us lets you search and score opportunities without writing code. The TypeScript SDK is there if you want it.

From A compliance officer.

Is this affiliated with the U.S. government?

No. OpenSAM is an independent open-source project built by AliceLabs LLC. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by SAM.gov, GSA, or any U.S. government agency.

From A software architect.

How does the scoring work?

Each opportunity receives a 0–100 score based on NAICS code match, set-aside alignment, capability keywords in the description, and deadline proximity. Higher is better.
